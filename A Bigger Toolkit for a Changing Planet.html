A bigger toolkit for a changing planet

Climate change is no longer a distant threat. It is reshaping the systems that regulate life on Earth. Here's what's destabilizing, and the new science that could help us respond.

1 · The bottom line 2 · A new type of problem 3 · A bigger toolbox 4 · Why now 5 · We've done it before How to get involved

The bottom line

We have already breached the world's 1.5 °C warming limit,1 and we are tracking toward a dangerously plausible 2.5–3 °C future.2

Despite real progress on cutting emissions, human pollution has set off a chain reaction. From clouds to wetlands, and from glaciers to ocean currents, critical Earth systems are destabilizing and taking on a runaway momentum of their own.

Scientists warn that the window to stabilize them is closing. Without urgent action, they could drive temperatures hotter, raise seas higher, and put water and food at risk for billions of people.

But humanity has a long record of solving problems that once looked unsolvable. We closed the ozone hole, eradicated smallpox and nudged an asteroid off course.3 Earth system stabilization research applies that same problem-solving ambition to the planet's most dangerous instabilities.

Key idea

The tools to understand, and potentially manage, these risks are newly within reach. Yet this research receives only tens of millions of dollars a year. That gap is a tremendous opportunity for philanthropy to launch entirely new fields with planet-scale impact.

A new type of problem

Scientists increasingly warn of non-linear risks: feedback loops and tipping points that could trigger sudden, irreversible shifts in planetary systems. Those shifts could, in turn, destabilize our political, economic and social systems.4

Higher temperatures

Earth now absorbs far more solar energy than before, because cloud cover is declining5 and so is sea ice.6 Both normally act like a white sun hat for the planet. The warming from this extra sunlight now equals the warming from all human carbon dioxide emissions since 1750,7 and over the last 20 years it has grown faster than models predicted.

Rising temperatures are also increasing emissions from ecosystems. Wetlands,8 permafrost9 and forest fires10 could amplify global warming by up to 30%, adding an extra 0.2–0.4 °C of warming by 2100.11

Rapid sea level rise

Experts now expect 2 to 7 feet of sea level rise by 2100, which could displace hundreds of millions of people.12 More than 90% of the risk and uncertainty comes from instability in Thwaites Glacier,13 which can no longer be stabilized just by lowering emissions14 or atmospheric temperatures.15

Water and food insecurity

Mountain glaciers are "water towers." They supply drinking and irrigation water for more than 20% of humanity.16 Yet even at 1.5 °C, they are projected to lose more than a quarter of their mass this century.17

Meanwhile the AMOC is weakening and could pass a point of no return in coming decades. That would disrupt Asian and West African monsoons18 and shift the tropical rainbelt southward,19 moving rain away from places that grow food.

For decades, climate strategy has rested on two approaches: mitigating emissions and adapting to change. Both remain urgent and essential, but we can no longer assume they will be enough. We now need a third: stabilization.

A bigger toolbox than we thought

Climate risks may be worse than most people recognize. The good news is that the toolbox to address them may be bigger and more promising than we thought. A decade ago much of it was theoretical. Today, frontier teams are turning it into tractable science and engineering.

🛰️ Seeing what's coming

New satellite observations, AI-enabled modeling and next-generation climate models can sharpen our understanding of key instabilities, forecast the likelihood and timing of tipping points, and identify the most promising ways to avert or stabilize them.

☀️ Restoring Earth's reflectivity

Researching approaches like spreading tiny reflective particles in the stratosphere, brightening marine clouds so they bounce more sunlight back to space, or protecting sea ice and snow.

🧊 Stabilizing glaciers

Pumping heat or water out from underneath glaciers so they refreeze to the rock beneath them, slowing their slide into the sea.

❄️ Slowing Arctic sea ice loss

Thinning the insulating clouds that form over the Arctic in winter, letting more heat escape to space so more ice can grow.

🌿 Curbing nature's emissions

Actively managing ecosystems like permafrost and wetlands, and speeding up the natural breakdown of methane in the atmosphere.

Research areas described in this explainer. Each is at an early stage; none is ready to deploy.

Research, not deployment

None of this research is a call to deploy. It is a call to build the knowledge, tools and governance now, so that future decisions are made on robust evidence rather than in an uninformed rush during a crisis.

Why philanthropy, why now

Stabilization research is one of the most neglected areas in climate. Total climate finance exceeds $1 trillion a year. Research on stabilizing Earth systems receives tens of millions.

The field is ready to put philanthropic funding to work. High-caliber teams can use new dollars immediately, and small amounts of capital can spark new research programs. Philanthropy can move at a speed governments can't, in areas private markets won't, and it can help galvanize government leadership on stabilization research and governance.

The window is short. Ice sheets and ocean currents run on their own clocks. Research now preserves our ability to act wisely later.

What to remember

Earth systems are gaining their own momentum. Clouds, sea ice, wetlands, permafrost, glaciers and ocean currents are shifting in ways that add warming, raise seas and threaten water and food.
Mitigation and adaptation may not be enough on their own. Stabilization is a third pillar: research into understanding and managing the most dangerous instabilities.
The research is ready, and badly underfunded. Tens of millions a year against a trillion-dollar climate economy means small, fast philanthropic dollars can open entire new fields.

We've done the impossible before

The hardest planetary problems we have faced were solved by people who refused to accept them as permanent.

When scientists found a hole in the ozone layer, the world developed substitute chemicals and agreed to phase out the culprits. The ozone layer is now on a path to recovery. Smallpox, which killed hundreds of millions, was eradicated through vaccines and global coordination. In 2022, NASA's DART mission proved that humanity can change the orbit of an asteroid.3 When COVID-19 arrived, mRNA vaccines went from lab to arms in under a year, built on decades of earlier research that was ready when it was needed.22 Each looked impossible until the research had been done.

Climate progress itself offers proof. Solar power, batteries and electric vehicles were once dismissed as niche. Now they are among the cheapest and fastest-growing energy technologies on Earth: a positive tipping point driven by sustained investment in innovation.

The history of climate strategy follows a familiar arc, too. Adaptation, methane cuts and carbon removal were each first dismissed as a moral hazard or a distraction, then researched, then normalized. Stabilization research is on the same path.

Key idea

Humanity has already changed the planet's warming trajectory, just by accident. When 2020 shipping rules cut sulfur pollution, they cleaned the air but also removed a cooling haze,20 likely speeding up warming.21 If accidents can shift Earth's systems this much, deliberate, careful science can help us learn how to stabilize them.

The instabilities are real, but so is our ability to solve hard problems. The question is whether we invest in that ability before we need it.
